    text: >-
      My research aims to understand the evolutionary relationships of plants and the processes and mechanisms underlying their origin, geographic distribution, and diversification. I am particularly interested in phylogenetic approaches that use molecular data to infer evolutionary relationships and reconstruct the history of plant lineages.  

      By combining fieldwork and the study of biological collections, I also gather information on geographic distribution, morphological and cytological variation, and ecological attributes. These different sources of evidence are integrated to address key questions in plant evolution, systematics, and taxonomy, including species boundaries, patterns of diversification, and the historical processes that have shaped plant diversity.  

      My primary expertise is in Cactaceae, particularly the tribe Opuntieae. However, my research interests are not restricted to these groups, and I am also interested in exploring evolutionary questions across other plant lineages.

    text: >-
      Floristic research provides the fundamental knowledge needed to understand how plant diversity is distributed across landscapes and how regional floras have been assembled through time. With particular emphasis on southern South America, this work combines field observations, herbarium collections, taxonomic evidence, and geographic distribution data to document species diversity, refine inventories, and improve the reliability of occurrence records. These approaches are especially important in regions where many species remain poorly collected, inadequately identified, or known from only a small number of localities.

      Beyond producing species lists, floristic studies help reveal patterns of endemism, biogeographic connections, range limits, and areas of exceptional or insufficiently known diversity. By clarifying species identities and distributions, they also provide a stronger basis for ecological and evolutionary research, environmental assessments, and conservation planning. In this way, floristic research helps transform scattered records into a coherent picture of plant diversity and identifies the species, regions, and habitats that most urgently require further study and protection.

    text: >-
      Edible plant diversity represents an important yet still underexplored resource for food security, cultural resilience, and the diversification of agricultural systems. This research focuses on neglected and underutilized species, non-conventional food plants (PANC), and wild relatives of cultivated crops, integrating botanical, taxonomic, ecological, and evolutionary knowledge to better understand their diversity, distribution, relationships, and potential uses. Many of these plants have long been recognized by local communities but remain poorly documented, scientifically overlooked, or absent from mainstream food systems.

      By bringing attention to these underrepresented plant resources, such studies can contribute to the development of more diverse, resilient, and regionally adapted food systems. They also help identify species with nutritional, ecological, or genetic value, support the conservation of wild and traditionally managed plant diversity, and strengthen the connection between biodiversity research and society. In this way, the study of edible plants extends beyond cataloguing useful species, providing a basis for conservation, crop improvement, sustainable use, and the recognition of biological and cultural knowledge associated with food.
